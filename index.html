Talking points drafter

The chief-of-staff team wrote talking points for every leadership event by hand. Open last year’s file, copy the shape, look up the numbers, fix the voice. Most of that work repeats.

I set them up in the AI tool they already use. First I reverse engineered the past talking points and the house template: how a page opens and closes, how long the bullets run, which sections come back every time. Those became written rules and reusable blocks. Numbers, names, and dates come from what the user drops in for this event. Last, a check runs before the draft is shown, and whatever the user asked to add for this event goes in.

Stack

  • Enterprise AI project, Claude or ChatGPT
  • 6 ranked artifacts: role, voice, blocks, output contract, template, workflow
  • 6 rule types, audited separately
  • Python skill: house template render
  • Silent self-check before the draft
  • Completion list, outside the file

Virtual PMO

A PMO team ran a reporting factory every month: chase emails, collection calls, copy-paste consolidation, slide building. I rebuilt it from scratch as a virtual PMO on the Microsoft stack. A Copilot Studio agent in Teams collects updates where they already happen. AI Builder reads meeting minutes and pulls out who said what and what was decided. Dataverse holds the record, Power Automate runs the flows, Power Apps gives the PMO team a control plane, and the deck is generated from signed rows. Meeting minutes turned out to be the gold mine. The real design work was the data model: what is master data, what is a monthly row, what is evidence, and where sign-off lives. Human effort moved to review and sign-off.

Stack

  • Copilot Studio agent in Teams
  • Dataverse, 8 tables
  • Power Automate flows
  • Power Apps control plane
  • AI Builder extraction
  • Generated PowerPoint and PDF

Pre-meeting brief

A common pattern for a chief-of-staff team, and for sales communication: pull structured and unstructured sources into one brief a leader can use. I have built it three ways. Custom development, an API pipeline (the IR memo agent, public code). A productivity tool, an enterprise AI project set up as a jumpstarter for the chief-of-staff team. A chatbot front door with MCP into the warehouse, for a sales desk. Same pattern each time. What decided quality was context management and knowing exactly what output the business wanted, down to tone, style, and density. Those are the parts you customize. The pipeline stays the same.

Stack

  • Enterprise AI project, no code
  • Policy: instructions + writing rules
  • Form: template + precedent + standards
  • Evidence skills: source check, deep read, client brief
  • IR version: FastAPI + Claude API

Hoteling automation

I built an office booking workflow that starts with an Outlook email. AI extracts the request, the employee directory resolves the person, and configured rules decide which room or desk they can use. When seating is full, the flow handles eligible reassignments, updates the booking records, and notifies the people affected. Exceptions go to the executive assistant team.

What this changes. Automates the chain of coordination behind a booking: allocating space, moving eligible bookings, maintaining the records, and telling people what changed. The requester stays in Outlook, while the EA team handles exceptions that require a person’s decision.

Stack

  • Outlook trigger and notifications
  • AI Builder: email to booking fields
  • Microsoft Entra ID: identity and title
  • Power Automate: rules, bump, cascade
  • SharePoint lists: settings, tier map, resources, bookings

AI Investment Map

About 130 initiatives on one screen. A fictional bank’s AI portfolio, sized by spend and colored by status, with a timeline you can scrub.

Before

A board asks 3 questions about AI: how much have we spent, is it working, where do we need to decide. With initiatives tracked by hand in spreadsheets, nobody could answer from one screen.

After

A treemap nested by business unit and division. Click an initiative for cumulative spend against benefit, with go-live, break-even, and “now” markers. Drag the timeline and the whole map rescales.

What changed

I hand-rolled the squarified treemap instead of importing a chart library. That one decision made nested regions, drill-down transitions, semantic zoom, and smooth rescaling possible. The data comes from a seeded generator, so every number is reproducible and none is real.

Stack

  • React 19
  • Vite 7
  • Tailwind CSS 4
  • Custom squarify
  • Vercel

What I learned

Writing the layout algorithm myself cost more up front and paid for itself the first time I needed a transition no chart library offered.

What I want to share

If the visualization is the product, own the layout code. Libraries are fine for a chart inside a dashboard. They fight you the moment the chart is the dashboard.

Brief Me

A COO in a line of business runs their own numbers. Pull the report, cut it by division, explore it, find what matters, then build the PowerPoint and write the message for leadership.

This reverses the order. The COO filters to their division and the dashboard runs the metrics then reads its own results: what moved, what stalled, where the division stands against the adoption goal. Findings come back with the charts. Ask a follow-up in plain English and get the number, and a short read of what it means. The deck exports at the end.

Stack

  • Next.js 14
  • Snowflake
  • Cortex Analyst + COMPLETE
  • Semantic model, YAML
  • Tremor + AG Grid
  • PowerPoint export

AI training impact

I investigated whether completing AI training was followed by a change in how people used AI tools. I built a Python pipeline to connect course records with weekly usage, compare behavior before and after training, and bring the findings into a Streamlit dashboard. This case study recreates the approach with entirely synthetic data.

Stack

  • Python, pandas, SciPy
  • Parquet, local only
  • Streamlit, 3 tabs
  • Event-time alignment
  • Difference-in-differences
  • Wilcoxon, Mann-Whitney, bootstrap interval
